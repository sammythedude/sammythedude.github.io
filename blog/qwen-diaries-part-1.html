Qwen Diaries Part 1

May 31, 2026 · interpretability · When does a small model choose its source?

This is a spinoff from my workshop paper Hidden Commitment, my first ever piece of work and my firstborn. If you want the primary results that these experiments build on, I really recommend reading that one first.

A note on why I work in a small model. Larger models, I learned the hard way, are really hard to read. They often develop specializations, since more dimensions let them cram more features into superposition, and longer chains of attention heads and MLPs let them compose more complex functions (see Anthropic's A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits). So a single task may not be cleanly causally linked to one simple direction or subspace. In my previous paper, during the answer span the model basically represented the whole decision with a single direction, a strong readout that also flipped which source it answered from when I steered along it.

This blog is mostly about my journey trying to figure out when a small language model decides between choices, during its own token by token generation. I am not sure I have actually found the answer. I tried to come at it from a couple of different directions, the logit competition and the residual readout, and to see whether the two agree.

The setup is simple. One question, several documents that quietly disagree, each one supporting a different answer. The model reads all of them and commits to one. I want to know what that commitment looks like on the inside.

In my original paper this choice, for two documents, was a single direction with a very strong readout. In Qwen 0.8B, when two documents disagree, which source the answer actually uses is captured by one residual direction whose sign tells you the chosen document. Sparse middle layer MLP writers feed that same coordinate, and when you steer the coordinate the model flips which source it answers from. So the whole decision lived on one signed axis you could both read off the activations and push on.

But I had a question. What happens when there is more than one feature, more than two choices? I took inspiration from Goodfire's geometric interpretability work, the argument that a clean linear feature is usually just a slice through a curved representation manifold, and went looking for how a multi-way source choice is represented.

01 Is it still just one direction?

In the two document case the choice was a single axis, so the obvious first try with three documents was to reuse that idea. I learned an axis between Doc 1 and Doc 2 and asked where Doc 3 lands on it. That did not separate the answers well. When I instead learned the axis from the two far apart endpoints, Doc 1 and Doc 3, and dropped the middle document onto it, the three answers ordered themselves cleanly. That small asymmetry was the first real hint. If a single straight axis were enough, it would not matter which pair you learned it from. The endpoint axis working while the adjacent pair failed told me the representation was not one line. It had some geometry to it.

I learned that endpoint axis only from the Doc 1 and Doc 3 forced residuals, then dropped the unseen Doc 2 onto it. Doc 2 landed in between the two ends in 94.7% of examples. A real ordering, not a relabeled binary split.

02 It needs more than one axis

If no single direction is universal, maybe the choice lives in a small subspace instead of on one line. The trick that made it show up was within question centering, subtracting each question's own mean so the analysis stops rediscovering which question you are on. After that, the three sources fall into clean islands.

03 A line, a plane, or a curve?

With three points you can always draw a triangle, so of course it looks structured. The question that made this project better was, what about five. Is it a line, a flat patch, or something curved. I fit line, plane and 3D structure to the five forced centroids. A line barely explained a third of it. You needed three dimensions before it fit.

Here are those five forced centroids in the first three principal components. This is the plot that made me stop trusting the flat version. In two dimensions Doc 4 and Doc 5 were almost on top of each other, a centroid distance of just 0.168. The third axis pulls them apart to 0.468. The structure was always there, the 2D picture just hid it.

04 Does free choice land in the same place?

Everything above was teacher forced, where I forced the model to say each answer, a map of where each answer would put the model. The real test is whether the model walks into those same regions on its own. The distribution a model generates from can differ from the teacher forced one (see Jack Lindsey's work on the biology of a language model). For little Qwen 0.8B it did anyway, almost every time.

Of 38 classifiable natural answers, 92.1% landed nearest to the forced centroid of the document they actually chose. The map drawn by forcing is the same territory the model wanders into freely.

It mostly survives paraphrase too. I intuitively thought it might be an artifact of token similarity, but it holds for reworded answers, 84.2% still land in the right region, even though the fine grained position along the axis is much more fragile to surface form.

05 Does it hold up in bigger models?

To check it was not a tiny model quirk I ran Gemma 3 1B and Gemma 3 4B. This is where the sunshine and rainbows of small model readouts ended and it started getting more complicated. The forced geometry held in place, but the free generation readout did not transfer at the same layer and position. To be fair there were also fewer points to characterise the clusters. And oddly the cluster was actually more cleanly geometric in the larger model, which might itself be a model quirk. To really know if it holds I would need a lot more VRAM and a larger Qwen model to test whether the same geometry survives at scale.

You can see the mismatch directly. In both Gemma sizes the big filled forced centroids string out into the same kind of low dimensional shape. But the natural answers, the little crosses, do not snap to their matching centroid the way Qwen's did.

Filled markers are forced centroids, crosses are natural generations colored by the document the model chose. The forced shape survives, the natural crosses wander.

06 But when is it actually deciding?

Here is the question I should have asked first. All that clean geometry was measured during the answer tokens, the mean_decision readout. Is the choice already sitting in the residual stream at the last prompt token, the pre_response_last position, before the model says anything? Here is where those positions live in a single answer.

I read the choice out with a leave one question out SVM at each of those positions. The answer was nothing, then everything. I also thought maybe a stronger non linear separator could pick up a readout earlier, but the SVM only performed strongly at the decision token, the same as my linear probe. Neel Nanda was right, simple is usually good.

So there is no clean decision sitting in the activations before the model speaks. I wanted another way to tell where the model was computing or preparing the answer token. I intuitively thought the logit distribution during generation would tell me something about the answer, that the closer we get to the answer token the more the model would push up the logit of the answer it is about to pick. But the logits and the residual readouts turned out to look very similar.

That is the whole story in one widget. Before generation the residual state is undecided and the logits hold a soft, wobbling preference. The eventual answer does not even reliably beat its single strongest rival until a token before it is emitted. Then the answer token enters the context and the hidden state snaps into a source specific region.

I also wanted to follow one real run instead of an average. Here Qwen 0.8B answers a question where five documents each give a different answer. As it writes its sentence, I keep checking one thing at every word. Is the answer it eventually gives currently beating its strongest competitor, or losing to it? The chart below plots exactly that, the gap between the two.

The line tells you the gap, but not who is actually in front. So here is the same run again as a race between all five documents. Drag through it and watch the lead change hands, from the eventual answer, to a rival, and back at the very end.

That single run is not a special case. Averaged over all the questions, the eventual answer only becomes easy to read in the logits in the last token or two. The residual side, which I have cached for every position, tells the same story from the other direction. The choice is unreadable through the whole prompt, sitting at chance, and only snaps to readable once the answer tokens start, climbing to near perfect within the first four. The two clocks line up almost exactly.

07 What do I think?

In the end I am not sure I have found the answer to when a language model decides on an answer it is going to pick. It could be a weakness of my methodology. But the findings could also tell us that the machinery, in a small model at least, runs a series of computations and only spits out the decision around the answer tokens. In my case the model really is thinking out loud. There could be a confounding variable in parametric knowledge, but regardless, the geometry of the choice surface holds.

If we take that seriously, then source choice is not one universal vector. It is a low dimensional, curved manifold that you can read off the activations, but only once the model starts answering.

What I find genuinely interesting is that geometry like this usually shows up for concepts or ideas. Here it shows up for source selection, a computational choice the model makes when it compares documents for an answer. I think this is the most important finding here, that the representation of choice itself has geometry.
